When the way people get to you changes, what is left in your operation besides yesterday's number? Reaching and keeping are different jobs.

## Liking what you publish is not a reason to come back

Someone can like what we publish and still have no reason at all to come back. That is the difference I would like to make clear before any number shows up. Reaching many people and building a relationship with them are connected jobs, but they are not the same job.

I value reach.

Producing something relevant and not caring how that content finds people is also a limitation. But reaching someone does not, on its own, mean becoming part of that person's choices. The encounter happens; continuity is something else, and it does not come free with reach. For me, a relationship starts to gain substance when the person recognizes a contribution.

It helped them understand a subject, make a decision, widen a perspective, or feel represented without being manipulated. That recognition is what gives a reason to meet again, and no number of impressions produces it alone. So, beyond asking how many people we reach, I would ask what we are offering so that the encounter has continuity.

Is there a recognizable proposition?

Is there coherence between what we promise and what we deliver? Does the person find context, or just one more stimulus competing for their attention?

I do not want to choose between reach and relationship.

It is a false choice, and treating it as a choice is already the start of the mistake. I want strategy to connect the two: distribution has to open opportunities for encounters, and delivery has to give those encounters meaning. Growing, in this reading, is not only expanding the number of people we reach. It is also caring for the quality of the presence we manage to have in their lives.

> Reach opens the room; what decides whether someone stays is what they find once inside.

## What the 2026 data says, and what it does not

This data comes from the Digital News Report 2026, by the Reuters Institute. The research shows a shift in the paths people take to news and reinforces the importance of rethinking the relationship with the audience.

The finding I take as a starting point is direct.

Across the markets surveyed, social media and video platforms appear, for the first time, ahead of news outlets' own websites and apps as a way of accessing news online. It is a change of position, not a statistical detail. And that is why it deserves to be read carefully, not just celebrated or lamented.

> 54% and 51%: platforms overtake news sites and apps on average across 48 markets: it changes where part of your audience arrives from, not whom it prefers.

Before drawing any conclusion, it is worth saying what this number actually measures. It describes sources used in the previous week.

It is not an exclusive choice.

It is not each person's main source. It is not a Brazil-specific cut.

A person consumes news in several environments over a week, and showing up in more than one of them is the common behavior, not the exception. There is also a methodological limit the institute itself declares. The study works with online interviews and behavior reported by respondents.

The recommendation is to read the results as representative of the online population, with caveats across markets, and not as a complete record of what everyone does. Whoever uses the data without that care is measuring one thing and claiming another.

The number says nothing about share of revenue. It says nothing about total traffic.

It does not decree that the website has lost its function, nor that being on a platform is a mistake. It describes a consumption path, and a consumption path is not a verdict on your business. My strategic reading is that the data shifts attention to the conditions under which the relationship with the audience happens. The question it opens is not whether the website is dead. It is how much of our ability to keep reaching people depends on a decision that is not ours. The data describes the terrain. The responsibility for deciding what to do with it still belongs to whoever runs the operation.

> Vanity is not in the number. It is in what we hang on it.

## What slips out of your hands when the channel changes the rules

No operation sets, on its own, the rules of a platform, the distribution it will get, or the behavior of the people who consume it. Even in a more direct relationship, no one controls another person's will. That is why I would avoid talking about an audience as something we own.

We can build conditions for a relationship, but its continuity has to make sense for both sides. What stays within our sphere of decision is more than it seems. It is the quality of the delivery, the audience we prioritize, the editorial criteria, the way we learn from results and the level of dependence we accept. It is also up to us to decide whether to preserve knowledge, develop the team and create alternatives before a change becomes an emergency.

This has a human dimension that is usually left out of conversations about channels.

Faced with a drop, I would not start by assuming the team stopped working well. First, I would investigate whether the delivery changed, whether the distribution environment changed, or whether the way the audience responds changed. Each of these hypotheses calls for a different action, and treating all three as the same thing is like medicating a symptom without knowing the cause.

> Blaming the team for what the algorithm decided is the most unfair shortcut there is.

Not controlling the channel does not relieve us of the responsibility to understand the dependence. The mistake is not in using a platform well.

It is in organizing lasting commitments as if a favorable distribution condition were a permanent guarantee. The condition changes without warning. What is left, when it changes, is exactly what we chose to sustain while it was on our side.

## When opening a second front pays off

Being in more places is not a goal.

I would open a second front when I could explain three things before starting: what relationship it adds, what dependence it helps reduce, and how it sustains itself without compromising what already works. If any of those answers stays vague, the expansion is still a wish, not a decision.

Picture an analysis brand that has built a good presence with short videos. Part of the audience starts asking for context, references, a deeper reading.

Then testing a periodic newsletter may make sense, because there is an identifiable need, a complementary delivery and a reason for the person to choose to continue the relationship somewhere else. The reverse also needs saying. If the second front demands production the team cannot sustain, or simply repeats the same content to the same people without adding usefulness, I would question the expansion.

Repeating is not diversifying.

Taking up more space is not, by itself, serving better those who are already there.

My threshold would be a test with clear limits: available working time, a defined owner, a promise to the audience and continuity criteria agreed before publishing the first edition. I would watch adoption, return, quality of participation and the effort it demands from whoever produces it.

And I would not call a test successful if it grows at the expense of the main channel's quality or of an overload with no end. Diversifying pays off when it widens real possibilities for relationship, not when it merely spreads what already exists. Concentrating is still a good decision when it allows better delivery, deeper learning and serving an audience we are not yet serving as well as we could.

Both choices can be responsible. What is not responsible is deciding without asking which of them the operation's moment actually calls for.

## Relationship, access, capacity, alternatives, in that order

Before asking which platform to be on, I would start with a more uncomfortable question: what, in this relationship, deserves continuity? That is why I do not open with the tool. The order that helps me think is relationship, access, capacity and, only then, alternatives.

Whoever inverts this decides where to publish before knowing whether there is anything the audience wants to come back to. In the relationship, the question is simple and hard to answer honestly: why would someone come back? I would look for concrete signals, people who return, recognize the proposition, ask questions that deepen the conversation or recommend the content for a specific contribution.

A single compliment proves nothing.

What proves something is what repeats and makes sense for the person on the other side. On access, I would ask how that return happens.

Can the person find us on their own?

Do they know where the next delivery is?

Is there a path of continuity they chose themselves? What I want to measure here is how much of this relationship depends on being introduced again by the same distribution mechanism, rather than on a decision of theirs. On capacity, the focus shifts to the operation. Who sustains the delivery, with what knowledge, time and routine?

An editorial promise that can only be kept with exceptional effort every week needs to be revised. I do not consider sustainable a relationship with the audience built on the exhaustion of those who produce. That is not rigor, it is debt with a due date. Only then would I evaluate alternatives.

Which paths would be truly viable in the face of a change? Having an account open somewhere else does not solve it.

An alternative needs an audience, a proposition and execution capacity, or it is just a reassuring line in a report no one will be able to deliver on. What interests me in this sequence is that it can start without any confidential information.

It does not require a dashboard, a budget or access to another department's data. It requires, above all, honesty about what already exists and what is still only intention. If you want to apply it on your own, the question that opens each step is enough to expose where the operation is resting on thin air.

## When concentrating was the right choice

The strongest counterpoint to my own case for diversifying is this: concentration can produce a depth that dispersion would prevent. And I will not pretend it does not exist. An operation can know an audience very well, have a proposition suited to one platform and build a valuable relationship there. In that scenario, pulling attention, budget and editorial capacity to open other fronts can weaken precisely what sets it apart.

Spreading out is not, by itself, a gain.

Nor do I think it is reasonable to ask the audience to change environments just to meet our need for control. The person has to find an advantage in that change. If they prefer to consume, participate and return on that channel, that goes into the decision; it is not a behavior to correct.

So I would not defend diversification as an obligation. I would defend understanding the dependence.

One concentration can be conscious, monitored and consistent with the operation's moment. Another can exist simply because no one discussed what would happen if conditions changed. The two look alike in the reach report and are very different things.

> Concentrating is not necessarily reckless. Ignoring the risk of concentration is what worries me.

The question I would take to leadership would be this: are we concentrated because it is the best choice to serve our audience, or because we have not yet developed the capacity to choose otherwise? Each answer demands a different conversation, and neither fits in a reach metric.

## Being found opens the door, being sought is the choice

Being found and being sought do not compete with each other.

They are experiences that complement each other.

Being found is a legitimate opportunity: someone gets a recommendation, sees a post, searches for a subject and arrives at a brand they did not know. There is nothing lesser about that encounter. An important relationship can begin exactly like that. Being sought adds an intention.

The person remembers a contribution and decides to find it again.

And they can keep doing that inside the platform itself: there is no need to leave the network to show preference, just as visiting a website directly does not, on its own, prove trust. That is why I reject the reading that turns this into a fight between popularity and trust.

I think of someone who finds an analysis on a subject that affects their work. The text clears up a doubt without irresponsibly simplifying the problem.

When a new question comes up, that person remembers who helped them think. That continuity is what interests me, not a single day's peak of attention.

Nor would I treat being sought as an automatic certificate of quality. Someone can be sought a great deal out of controversy, curiosity or convenience.

The existence of a return proves nothing on its own.

What I would watch is the reason for the return, because that is what separates a conscious preference from a habit that dissolves as soon as a louder stimulus shows up. I want distribution to make us findable and contribution to make us memorable. The ambition is for an opportunity of attention to turn into preference, without devaluing any of the paths through which the relationship began.

## Reach is not vanity, a drop is not lost revenue

Reach has value when it answers to an intention.

Introducing a brand, taking useful information to more people, letting an argument find new interlocutors: these are legitimate goals. I do not consider a metric superficial just because it measures volume. What makes it misleading is using it to prove something it did not measure.

Vanity is not in the number.

It is in what we hang on it.

The same rigor applies to the other side of the pendulum.

A consumption indicator that falls is a snapshot of reported behavior in one week, averaged across markets. It is not a bank statement. Turning that slice, on its own, into a conclusion about a company's revenue seems to me a leap the data does not authorize.

It is one thing to observe that the path to the audience has changed. It is quite another to state that someone has lost revenue.

To discuss economic consequences, I would need other things on the table: the business model, the revenue involved, the costs, the period analyzed. A drop in consumption may represent a risk worth investigating. It does not, by itself, authorize declaring an equivalent financial loss.

Between symptom and diagnosis there is work that no reach report does on its own. What sustains reasoning like this is a separation I take seriously: what was observed, what I am interpreting and what still needs to be demonstrated.

Observing is describing what the data shows.

Interpreting is my reading of what that demands from whoever runs the operation. Demonstrating is another step, one that depends on information the data does not bring.

That is where the credibility of a text like this one is decided, not in the signature of whoever writes it. I do not want you to agree because you recognize a track record associated with my name. I want you to be able to follow the reasoning, challenge the premises and decide for yourself whether the conclusion holds.

If it does not hold, the problem is mine, not yours. The authority that interests me is the kind that withstands being examined.

## How far this reading holds outside news

The finding concerns news consumption in 2026, averaged across the markets surveyed. And not even within the report itself is the picture uniform: social media and video platforms beat news outlets' websites and apps as a source of news in 30 of the 48 markets. The average describes a trend, not an identical picture everywhere.

A trend does not erase local differences.

> What I consider legitimate to take to other sectors is not the answer, it is the question.

How much of our ability to reach, understand and reconnect with the audience depends on an intermediary? That question serves a services company, an educational institution, a consumer brand. But the answers have to come from the reality of each operation, not from behavior reported by people who read news. I would not use a reading about news to determine where a company in another sector should invest its attention.

Before that, I would need to understand the needs of that business's audience, the nature of the decision it makes, the frequency of the relationship and the paths it actually uses. Consuming information and consuming a service do not follow the same logic of return.

There is also a time limit I would not ignore.

I would not treat a 2026 snapshot as a permanent rule. The conditions that produced that number may change, and a strategic reading that refuses to be revised stops being a reading and becomes dogma. The question remains useful precisely because it admits that today's answer may not be tomorrow's. Responsible generalization preserves the question and rebuilds the answer.

That is the criterion I would offer.

The value of an analysis like this one is not in turning an observed behavior into an order valid for any business. It is in delivering a way of thinking each operation can test against its own dependence, with honesty about what already exists and what is still only assumption.

## The distribution choice that grows responsibly

Growing responsibly starts by giving the main distribution channel a clear function, not by opening fronts just to be in more places. The channel that already works needs a role the operation recognizes, and continuity needs to be something the audience chooses because it makes sense to them, not because they were pushed by artificial urgency.

For a brand whose contribution lies in analysis, I would test a periodic in-depth delivery, with voluntary sign-up, introduced from the channel that already works. I would not open a new front just to repeat the publication. The proposition would need to solve something the first encounter did not, and give the person a reason of their own to come back. Before starting, I would define who owns the delivery, what frequency can actually be kept and what effort is truly available.

I would track the ability to reach new people, but also return, participation and perceived usefulness. And I would keep open the possibility of adjusting or stopping, because a test without a stopping rule is not a test, it is a disguised commitment.

The responsibility criterion fits in one simple sentence: do not promise the audience an experience the operation can only deliver by continually sacrificing the team or lowering the rigor of the content. Reach and relationship are not opposites. What separates them is honesty about what the operation can honor after the door has been opened. I want to grow by widening the number of people for whom we make a difference, without losing the capacity to honor that relationship.

That is what distinguishes those who are found from those who are sought: one depends on the mechanism that distributed, the other depends on a contribution that stayed.

One question remains, the same one I would take to any leadership accountable for an audience: if the path to your audience changed tomorrow, what reason would they have to keep choosing you?
